Bermuda · Good Friday

Bermuda Good Friday kites

Good Friday in Bermuda is a kite day. Families build tissue-paper kites and, alongside them, eat codfish cakes and hot cross buns. The pairing is described by Bermudian sources as part of the day, not as an ancient single recipe with one official formula.

The kites

Go To Bermuda describes families building kites from sticks, string, and colored tissue in the days before Good Friday, then flying them at a beach, park, or club. Horseshoe Bay is one gathering place. The same site describes a Good Friday kite festival there, with kite competitions and local food, and says that gathering has spanned more than four decades.

Bermuda.com describes homemade Bermuda kites with long cloth tails, tissue, wood, and string, and says some are made to hum. It also says kites were once not flown until after 3pm. Bermuda.com’s note that British Army troops used kites while planning telegraph poles is that site’s account of how kite flying reached Bermuda. This page does not treat that story as a separate verified history.

What people eat

Go To Bermuda says the Good Friday combination is a hot cross bun with a warm codfish cake in the middle, dressed with whatever you like. It names cheese, lettuce, tomato, tartar sauce, ketchup, and mayonnaise as popular toppings. Hot cross buns are the British Easter bun. Bermuda.com calls them a direct import and describes local buns, some finished with an icing cross.

The National Museum of Bermuda groups codfish cakes, hot cross buns, and kite flying together as Bermudian Easter traditions, and traces the salt cod itself through Atlantic trade. Cod is not a fish caught in Bermuda’s water. Salted cod arrived by trade.

The Bermudian magazine describes a usual home pattern: soaked salt cod, mashed potato, onion, parsley, and thyme, bound with egg and fried, and says households disagree on the fish-to-potato ratio. Thyme is the herb that article treats as non-negotiable. There is no single island recipe.
