Norwegian Pinnekjøtt

Salty, tender lamb ribs with a light birch steam and a browned fat cap if you finish them under heat.

History

Pinnekjøtt is a western Norwegian Christmas dish of salted, dried lamb ribs steamed over birch sticks. It is steamed, not grilled. The sticks keep the meat above the water in the pot. Families buy the ribs already salted and dried. This page does not ask you to cure meat at home. A pot on a stove or at the edge of a fire is enough.

Cultural background

Western Norwegian Christmas tables are the home of this dish, with Bergen as a useful door into that coast. It is not a barbecue rib and not a fresh grilled chop. The drying happened before you bought it.

Why it tastes this way

Salt and drying concentrate lamb, and steam returns moisture without washing the seasoning away. Birch sticks add a faint wood note and hold the ribs out of the water. A last pass under high heat browns the fat. The Christmas timing is cultural: dark winter, preserved lamb, and a long table.

How it is made

Soak purchased salted lamb ribs, then steam them on birch sticks over water in a covered pot. Finish under heat if you want the fat browned. Do not grill them as the main cook.

Authentic touch

Soak shop-dried ribs in cold water before steaming, changing the water, or they will be too salty. Use birch sticks if you can get them, well scrubbed.

How it is used

With mashed swede or rutabaga, potatoes, and the cooking juices. It is a Christmas plate in the west of Norway.

Field notes

  • Pinnekjøtt is steamed over birch sticks, not grilled, and it is a western Norwegian Christmas dish.
  • The name refers to the sticks and the meat: the ribs sit above the water.

Ingredients

  • 4 lb purchased salted and dried lamb ribs
  • Cold water for soaking
  • A handful of birch sticks, or a rack if you cannot get birch
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • Water for the steaming pot

Steps

  1. Buy the ribs already salted and dried. Do not cure meat at home. Soak them in cold water in the refrigerator for about 12 hours, changing the water a few times, until the salt is pleasant rather than harsh.
  2. Lay birch sticks in a heavy pot over an inch of water, or use a rack. Set the ribs on the sticks, fat side up. Cover the pot.
  3. Steam over a stove or at the edge of a fire, not as a grill, until the meat is tender and the thickest part reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Give it a 3-minute rest.
  4. Pepper the ribs and serve with swede and potatoes. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A few minutes under a hot broiler after steaming, only to color the fat.
  • A pot set at the edge of a campfire, still as a steam, not as a grill.

Pairings

  • Mashed swede
  • Potatoes
  • Cooking juices
