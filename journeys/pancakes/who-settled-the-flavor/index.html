Flying Pancakes · Train

Who settled the flavor

A rail route across the United States, tasting the cakes settlers, immigrants, and older corn traditions left on the griddle.

This train does not hand the country to one kitchen. It stops where a cake can be tied to a real migration, a crop, or a dispute about the name.

Read the essay first if you want the argument in one place. The pancakes are the evidence.

Stops

  1. Hudson Valley buckwheat cakes · United States — Sour, earthy buckwheat with a molasses edge and a crisp griddle face.
  2. Pennsylvania Dutch potato pancakes · United States — Shredded potato, onion, and pepper, crisp at the edge and soft in the middle.
  3. Pennsylvania Dutch funnel cakes · United States — Crisp fried batter, lightly eggy, buried under powdered sugar.
  4. Rhode Island johnnycakes · United States — Toasted corn, salt, and a crisp edge, with almost no sweetness in the cake itself.
  5. Appalachian buckwheat cakes · United States — Earthly and slightly sour, with a gray-brown crumb and a sorghum finish if you use it at the table.
  6. Appalachian sorghum pancakes · United States — Malty and lightly bitter-sweet from sorghum, with a plain wheat crumb underneath.
  7. Southeastern corn griddle cakes · United States — Plain corn, salt, and a toasted surface, with no sugar and no wheat.
  8. Southern hoecakes · United States — Plain toasted corn, salt, and a fried edge, denser than a wheat pancake.
  9. Lowcountry rice griddle cakes · United States — Soft, lightly sweet rice cakes with a crisp buttered edge and separate grains in the crumb.
  10. New Orleans calas · United States — Fried rice fritters, lightly sweet, with nutmeg and a powdered-sugar coat.
  11. Upper Midwest Swedish pancakes · United States — Thin, eggy, and lightly sweet, rolled around butter and jam.
  12. Hopi piki · United States — Very thin toasted blue corn, delicate and dry enough to fold, with a mineral note if ash is used.
